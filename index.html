I work on power system operations and planning, mostly around one question: the grid was not built for gigawatt-scale data centers, and they are arriving faster than transmission can be built to serve them. But much of the computing behind them is flexible. Training jobs can be paused or moved between regions, so what if operators and planners treated these loads as something they can work with rather than a fixed burden?

On the operations side, I build unit commitment models that schedule data center load across time and locations; on our test systems this relieves transmission congestion and cuts renewable curtailment substantially. On the planning side, I study where the grid can host new data centers and how their load reshapes generation and transmission expansion. With collaborators at Argonne National Laboratory, I recently built a Monte Carlo framework that stress-tests expansion portfolios for the Texas grid against 30 different data center deployment futures, all 8,760 hours of the year, made tractable by GPU-based solvers that turn a 25-hour expansion solve into under two.

Before UH, I received an M.Eng. in Energy Systems from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and a B.Eng. (First Class Honours) in Electrical and Electronic Engineering from the University of Sheffield.
